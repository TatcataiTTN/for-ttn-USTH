Thermal infrared: principle

Layer emissionI ≈ B(Ts) e−τaer + B(Tlayer) (1 − e−τaer)
  • Lapse rate ≈ −6.5 K/km: higher layers are colder
  • Higher layer → lower brightness temperature
  • Signal ∝ τaer × (Ts − Tlayer)

Numerical example: layers at 1 km and 5 km

Thermal infrared: pros and cons

Advantages
• Day and night
• Over bright deserts
• Sensitive to coarse particles: dust, ash
• IASI, AIRS
Limitations
• Needs thermal contrast (fails for low layers, inversions, cold surfaces)
• Sensitive to surface emissivity
• Not suited to fine smoke or sulfate

The O₂ A-band (about 760 nm)

  • O₂ is well mixed with a known profile
  • High aerosol layer → photons reflected early
  • Short path through O₂ → weak absorption, shallow lines
  • Low layer → strong O₂ absorption

O₂ A-band: pros and cons

Advantages
• No a priori on the gas needed
• High horizontal resolution
• All aerosol types, including smoke and sulfate
• TROPOMI ALH ~0.5–1 km
Limitations
• Daytime only
• Needs sufficient AOD (> ~0.3–0.5)
• Sensitive to surface albedo
• Cloud screening; single layer assumed

Worked example: thermal contrast and O₂ path

  • TIR: surface 310 K, dust layer at 4 km → T ≈ 310 − 6.5×4 ≈ 284 K → contrast ≈ 26 K. The guide quotes a brightness-temperature depression of ≈ 7 K for a 10 µm AOD of 0.7.
  • Same layer at 1 km → contrast ≈ 6.5 K → depression ≈ 2 K (guide): low layers are hard to detect.
  • O₂ A-band: fraction of the O₂ column above a layer at z ≈ e−z/8 km: 6 km → 0.47, 1 km → 0.88 → a higher layer roughly halves the O₂ absorption seen.

Quick Q&A

Why the O₂ A-band and not a H₂O band?
O₂ is well mixed with a known profile; H₂O is highly variable.
Why does the TIR method fail at night over deserts?
Surface cooling and inversions make the layer as warm as the surface → no thermal contrast.
How does a bright surface affect the O₂ A-band method?
Many photons reach the surface (long path), diluting the aerosol signal.
How can the passive results be validated?
With lidar profiles (CALIOP, EarthCARE, ground lidars).
