Six sinks of air pollutants

OH oxidation
The main sink of reactive gases.
CO + OH → CO₂ + H
Photolysis
UV/visible photons break molecules.
NO₂ + hν → NO + O
Dry deposition
Uptake at the surface, no rain needed.
F = −vd·C
Wet deposition
Rainout (in-cloud) and washout (below cloud).
Soluble gases and aerosols
Heterogeneous chemistry
Reactions on particles or cloud droplets.
N₂O₅ → HNO₃
Transport out of the domain
Up to the stratosphere, then photolysis.
N₂O, CFCs

Lifetime and parallel sinks

Definitionτ = M / L    (steady state: τ = M / P)
Several sinks in parallel1/τ = 1/τchem + 1/τphot + 1/τdry + 1/τwet
First-order chemistryτchem = 1 / (k·[OH])

M: burden (mass or number of molecules). L: total loss rate. Loss rates add, so the total τ is always shorter than the fastest sink.

Picture it
A bath with several drains: the rates add; the times do not.

Example: lifetime of CO against OH

k ≈ 2×10−13 cm³ s−1
[OH] ≈ 1×106 cm−3
τ = 1/(k[OH]) = 5×106 s
≈ 58 days ≈ 2 months
  • Convert seconds to days: divide by 86 400
  • More OH (tropics) means a shorter CO lifetime
  • About 2 months is long enough for fire CO to cross an ocean
Parallel example
Chemistry 2 days, dry deposition 3 days, wet deposition 6 days: 1/τ = 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/6 = 1 → τ = 1 day.

Python check: τ(CO) = 57.87 days; parallel total = 1.000 day.

Lifetime versus transport time

Short-lived (hours – days)
The map ≈ a map of the sources: hotspots at cities, plants, ships, fires.
NO₂, SO₂, HCHO
Medium-lived (weeks – months)
Continental-scale plumes appear: fire CO, dust crossing the Atlantic.
Long-lived (years – centuries)
Well mixed: almost uniform, ~1% variation; high precision needed.
CO₂, CH₄, N₂O

Distance L ≈ U·τ (U ≈ 5–10 m/s). Mixing around a latitude band takes weeks; between hemispheres ~1 year.

Lifetime vs scale of variability

Reading τ from a plume

τ = L / U = 50 000 m / 5 m s−1 = 104 s ≈ 2.8 h

Measure the e-folding length L of the NO₂ plume; fit C(x) ∝ exp(−x/(U·τ)).

First-order decay curves

At t = τ, e−1 ≈ 36.8% remains; half is lost after t½ = τ ln 2 ≈ 0.69 τ.

Worked example: lifetime of NO₂ from a satellite plume

  • TROPOMI NO₂ line density downwind of a power plant falls to 1/e at x = 72 km; wind U = 5 m/s = 18 km/h.
  • τ = x / U = 72 / 18 = 4 hours — a typical daytime NO₂ lifetime.
  • The same SO₂ molecule lives ~1–2 days in the boundary layer but months in the stratosphere after a volcanic eruption (no rain, little cloud chemistry): lifetime depends on where the molecule is.

Quick Q&A

Why does SO₂ live longer in the stratosphere?
No wet deposition, no clouds for aqueous oxidation, slower OH reaction.
Can a short-lived species appear everywhere on a map?
Only if its sources are everywhere (e.g. HCHO over all forests): the map still shows sources.
What is steady state?
Production = loss, burden constant, so τ = M/P = M/L.
Why is CO a good tracer of long-range transport?
τ ≈ 2 months: longer than intercontinental transport (1–2 weeks) but shorter than hemispheric mixing, so plumes stay visible.
