M8 · Aerosol altitude
Retrieving aerosol altitude with passive sensors · Study guide Chapter 8
Exam question 8 (original wording)
  • Explain the principle to derive the altitude of aerosols employing passive remote sensing from space: (i) in the thermal infrared and (ii) in the near infrared.
Contents: Why measure height, and how? · (i) Thermal infrared (IASI, AIRS: mainly desert dust, volcanic ash) · (ii) Near infrared: the O₂ A-band (TROPOMI ALH, EPIC, GOSAT, OCO-2) · Worked example, Q&A, memory hooks · Exam checklist · Slides · Quiz

Why measure height, and how?

Knowing where aerosol sits vertically matters for air quality (surface PM or elevated transport), long-range transport, radiative effects and aviation safety (volcanic ash). A passive sensor cannot measure height directly; it infers it indirectly from a physical effect that depends on altitude.

(i) Thermal infrared (IASI, AIRS: mainly desert dust, volcanic ash)

In the TIR the radiance is dominated by emission. Coarse aerosols (dust, ash) absorb and emit in the 8–12 µm atmospheric window (silicate band near 9–10 µm). A dust layer replaces part of the warm surface emission by its own emission at the layer temperature:

Emission with a layerI ≈ B(Ts) · e−τaer + B(Tlayer) · (1 − e−τaer)
  • Temperature decreases with altitude (≈ −6.5 K/km in the troposphere) → the higher the layer, the colder it is, the lower the TOA brightness temperature (larger depression relative to clear sky).
  • The signal depends on τaer × (Ts − Tlayer), so AOD and height are degenerate: retrieve them together, using the spectral shape in the window and the temperature profile from the CO₂ bands.
  • Retrieval: radiative transfer forward model (or LUT / neural network) with IR aerosol optical properties → 10 µm AOD and mean layer altitude.

AdvantagesLimitations
Day and night; over bright deserts (where visible methods fail); sensitive to coarse particlesNeeds thermal contrast (fails when layer T ≈ surface T: low layers, night-time inversions); sensitive to surface emissivity; mainly absorbing dust/ash, not fine smoke or sulfate

Related technique: CO₂ slicing / brightness-temperature differences (11–12 µm) for volcanic ash and cloud-top height.

(ii) Near infrared: the O₂ A-band (TROPOMI ALH, EPIC, GOSAT, OCO-2)

Oxygen is well mixed (21%, following pressure). In the A-band (759–771 nm) O₂ absorption depends on the photon path length. Aerosols scatter photons back to space before they reach the lower atmosphere:

  • High layer → photons reflected high up → short path through O₂ (little O₂ above the layer) → weak absorption (shallow lines).
  • Low layer → photons go deep → strong O₂ absorption.
  • The ratio of radiance inside the band to the continuum (755 nm), or a full spectral fit, depends on the scattering-layer pressure; lines of different strength give different penetration depths, so the band shape carries information on the vertical distribution.
ModelR(λ) ≈ Raer(λ) · exp( −σO₂(λ) · NO₂(above layer) · AMF ) + …

AdvantagesLimitations

Other passive methods: stereo/multi-angle parallax (MISR, height of fire and volcanic plumes); the height dependence of UVAI; the O₂–O₂ collision complex at 477 nm (OMI).

Worked example, quick Q&A and memory hooks

Worked example · thermal contrast and O₂ path
  1. TIR: surface 310 K, dust layer at 4 km → T ≈ 310 − 6.5×4 ≈ 284 K → contrast ≈ 26 K. The guide quotes a brightness-temperature depression of ≈ 7 K for a 10 µm AOD of 0.7.
  2. Same layer at 1 km → contrast ≈ 6.5 K → depression ≈ 2 K (guide): low layers are hard to detect.
  3. O₂ A-band: fraction of the O₂ column above a layer at z ≈ e−z/8 km: 6 km → 0.47, 1 km → 0.88 → a higher layer roughly halves the O₂ absorption seen.
  4. Check: our blackbody-layer model (no scattering, no water vapour, emissivity 1 − e−τ) gives 12.3 K at 4 km and 3.2 K at 1 km for τ = 0.7 — larger than the guide’s order-of-magnitude numbers, because real dust, water vapour and the atmospheric window reduce the signal. The trend (higher layer → larger depression, about 3–4× between 1 and 4 km) is the same.

Quick Q&A

Q: Why the O₂ A-band and not a H₂O band?
A: O₂ is well mixed with a known profile; H₂O is highly variable.
Q: Why does the TIR method fail at night over deserts?
A: Surface cooling and inversions make the layer as warm as the surface → no thermal contrast.
Q: How does a bright surface affect the O₂ A-band method?
A: Many photons reach the surface (long path), diluting the aerosol signal.
Q: How can the passive results be validated?
A: With lidar profiles (CALIOP, EarthCARE, ground lidars).

Memory hooks

  • TIR: guess the height of a mountain from the temperature at its top (the higher, the colder). O₂ band: guess the depth at which light “bounces back” from the amount of O₂ (like ink) it has travelled through.TIR: đoán độ cao ngọn núi bằng nhiệt độ; dải O₂: đoán độ sâu nơi ánh sáng bật lại.

Exam checklist — points you must mention

  • TIR: emission at layer temperature; T(z) decreases with height → higher layer = colder = lower brightness temperature; needs thermal contrast; works day/night; dust/ash.
  • NIR O₂ A-band: well-mixed O₂ absorber; higher aerosol layer shortens photon path → weaker O₂ absorption; daytime, sensitive to surface albedo.
  • Both retrieve height together with AOD by fitting spectra with an RT model / optimal estimation.
Key points (Vietnamese summary from the guide)
  • Hồng ngoại nhiệt: lớp bụi phát xạ theo nhiệt độ của chính nó; nhiệt độ giảm theo độ cao → lớp càng cao càng lạnh → nhiệt độ sáng đo được càng thấp. Cần tương phản nhiệt với bề mặt; đo được cả đêm và trên sa mạc.
  • Cận hồng ngoại (dải A của O₂, 760 nm): O₂ phân bố đều và đã biết; lớp aerosol càng cao → photon bị phản xạ sớm → đường đi qua O₂ ngắn → hấp thụ O₂ yếu hơn. Chỉ ban ngày, nhạy với albedo bề mặt.
  • Cả hai đều truy hồi đồng thời độ cao và AOD bằng mô hình truyền bức xạ.
