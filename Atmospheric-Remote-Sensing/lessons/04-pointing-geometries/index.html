Pointing geometries of spaceborne sensors · Study guide chapter 4
Exam question 4 (original wording)
  • What are the possible pointing geometries of spaceborne sensors for sounding the atmospheric composition?
  • What are the advantages and disadvantages of each case?

Three geometries, in plain words

The same atmosphere can be viewed in three ways: looking straight down, like a photo from an aircraft (nadir); looking sideways across the edge, like viewing a slice of cake from the side (limb); and looking at the rising or setting Sun through the atmosphere (occultation).

GeometryHow it looksExample instruments
Nadir (and off-nadir/cross-track)Looks down; measures backscattered sunlight (UV-vis-SWIR) or thermal emission (TIR)TROPOMI, OMI, GOME-2, IASI, MOPITT, OCO-2
LimbLooks tangentially above the horizon, scanning tangent heights ~5 to 100 km; measures scattered light or thermal emissionOSIRIS, OMPS-LP, SCIAMACHY limb (scattering); MIPAS, MLS (emission)
OccultationLooks at the Sun, Moon, a star or GNSS signals through the edge at sunrise/sunset; measures I/I₀SAGE II/III, ACE-FTS, GOMOS (stars), GNSS radio occultation

Advantages and disadvantages

GeometryAdvantagesDisadvantages
Nadir• Good horizontal resolution (a few km; TROPOMI 5.5×3.5 km²), daily global coverage
• Sees the troposphere and boundary layer → air quality, emissions
• Day (UV-vis) and day and night (TIR)		• Poor vertical resolution (a column or 1–6 pieces of information)
• Sensitive to surface albedo/emissivity, clouds, aerosols
• Short path → weak signal per layer
Limb• Good vertical resolution (1–3 km) from geometry
• Long path → very sensitive, many stratospheric species
• No surface background (dark space)
• Emission limb: day and night		• Poor horizontal resolution along the line of sight (~200–400 km)
• No view below ~5–10 km (clouds, saturation) → not for the boundary layer
• Complex RT (sphericity, multiple scattering); pointing known very precisely
Occultation• Self-calibrating: I/I₀ cancels drift → very accurate, good for trends
• Strong solar signal, ~1 km vertical, high S/N
• Simple RT (Beer–Lambert)		• Very poor coverage: ~30 profiles/day (2 per orbit for the Sun)
• Latitude bands depend on the orbit; poor horizontal along the path
• Only upper troposphere and stratosphere

Orbits matter too

Geometry is only half of the story; the orbit is the other half:

  • Sun-synchronous LEO: global coverage once per day at a fixed local time.
  • Geostationary (GEMS, TEMPO, Sentinel-4): nadir observation of one continent every hour → the diurnal cycle of pollution (rush hours, photochemistry) and more cloud-free scenes.
  • Nadir + limb (SCIAMACHY, OMPS): tropospheric column = total column (nadir) − stratospheric column (limb).
Choose by goal
Most accurate stratospheric ozone trend → solar occultation. NO₂ emissions of individual cities → nadir. Detailed stratospheric chemistry profiles → limb.

Exam checklist — points you must mention

  • Three geometries: nadir, limb (scattering/emission), occultation (solar/stellar/lunar/GNSS).
  • Trade-off: horizontal resolution & tropospheric sensitivity (nadir) ↔ vertical resolution & accuracy (limb/occultation).
  • Occultation = self-calibrating, best accuracy, worst coverage.
  • Draw the three geometries with Earth, atmosphere shell, lines of sight.
Key points (Vietnamese summary from the guide)
  • Nadir: nhìn thẳng xuống → độ phân giải ngang tốt, phủ toàn cầu hằng ngày, nhìn thấy tầng đối lưu; nhưng độ phân giải thẳng đứng kém, nhạy với mây/bề mặt.
  • Limb: nhìn tiếp tuyến qua rìa khí quyển → độ phân giải thẳng đứng tốt (1–3 km), không có nền bề mặt; nhưng độ phân giải ngang kém, không xuống thấp dưới ~5–10 km.
  • Che khuất (occultation): nhìn Mặt Trời/sao qua rìa khí quyển → tự hiệu chuẩn, rất chính xác; nhưng số lượng phép đo rất ít.
