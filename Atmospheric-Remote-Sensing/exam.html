Question 1 (2.5 pts) — review Ch. 1

(a) Define the atmospheric lifetime τ of a pollutant. How are lifetimes combined when several removal processes act in parallel?

(b) The rate constant of CO + OH is k ≈ 2×10⁻¹³ cm³ molecule⁻¹ s⁻¹ and the mean OH concentration is ≈ 1×10⁶ molecule cm⁻³. Estimate the lifetime of CO with respect to OH (give the result in days).

(c) A TROPOMI map shows NO₂ concentrated in small hotspots over cities, while a map of CH₄ from the same instrument is almost uniform. Explain the difference.

Show suggested answer key

(a) τ = burden / loss rate (M / L); for first-order sinks τ = 1/k′. Parallel sinks: 1/τ = Σ 1/τᵢ (chemistry, photolysis, dry and wet deposition).

(b) τ = 1/(k[OH]) = 1/(2×10⁻¹³ × 1×10⁶) = 5×10⁶ s ≈ 58 days ≈ 2 months.

(c) NO₂ lives hours → destroyed before transport spreads it → map = sources. CH₄ lives ~10 years ≫ global mixing time (~1 yr) → well mixed, variations of ~1 %.

Question 2 (2.5 pts) — review Ch. 2

(a) Write the Beer–Lambert law for a beam crossing an absorbing and scattering medium. Define optical depth and transmittance.

(b) Explain Kirchhoff’s law and why it matters for thermal-infrared remote sensing.

(c) A beam crosses a layer with vertical optical depth τ = 0.5 at a solar zenith angle of 60°. What fraction of the direct beam is transmitted?

Show suggested answer key

(a) dI = −k_e I ds, I = I₀ e^(−τ), τ = ∫(k_a + k_s) ds = ∫ Σσᵢnᵢ ds; transmittance T = I/I₀ = e^(−τ).

(b) In LTE, emissivity = absorptivity at each wavelength (ε_λ = α_λ): a gas that absorbs at λ also emits B_λ(T) there → TIR spectra carry information on both concentration and temperature.

(c) Slant τ = 0.5 / cos 60° = 1.0 → T = e^(−1) ≈ 0.37 (37 %).

Question 3 (2.5 pts) — review Ch. 3

(a) Explain the three main steps of a DOAS retrieval of the tropospheric NO₂ column from a nadir UV–visible spectrometer.

(b) Why does the air mass factor depend on the a priori shape of the vertical profile and on clouds?

(c) Give two physical reasons why a nadir sounder can obtain some information on the vertical distribution of a gas.

Show suggested answer key

(a) 1) Spectral fit of ln(I₀/I) with lab cross-sections + polynomial → slant column SCD. 2) Remove stratospheric part. 3) Divide by tropospheric AMF (RT model) → VCD.

(b) AMF = SCD/VCD measures the light-path length; sensitivity varies with altitude (low near surface, shielded below cloud, enhanced above) → the result depends on where the gas is assumed to be.

(c) Absorption strength varies with λ (different penetration depths); pressure broadening of lines (TIR); temperature contrast; (limb geometry for limb sounders).

Question 4 (2.5 pts) — review Ch. 4

(a) Sketch the nadir, limb and solar occultation geometries.

(b) Which geometry would you choose to (i) monitor long-term trends of stratospheric ozone with the highest accuracy, (ii) map NO₂ emissions from individual cities? Justify.

(c) What does a geostationary orbit add compared to a low-Earth sun-synchronous orbit for air-quality monitoring?

Show suggested answer key

(a) See Figure “Nadir, limb and occultation viewing geometries” in the study guide (Figure 13).

(b) (i) Solar occultation: self-calibrating (I/I₀), high vertical resolution → trend quality. (ii) Nadir: km-scale horizontal resolution, daily global coverage, sensitive to the boundary layer.

(c) Hourly revisit over one continent → diurnal cycle of pollution (rush hours, photochemistry), more cloud-free scenes. LEO: one overpass per day at fixed local time, but global.

Question 5 (2.5 pts) — review Ch. 5

(a) Write the relation between the retrieved profile, the true profile and the a priori using the averaging kernel matrix.

(b) A retrieval of CO from a thermal-infrared sounder has DOFS = 1.2. What does this mean? Where in the atmosphere is the sensitivity usually highest, and why?

(c) Explain how averaging kernels must be used when comparing satellite retrievals with a chemistry-transport model.

Show suggested answer key

(a) x̂ = x_a + A(x_true − x_a) + Gε; A = ∂x̂/∂x = GK; DOFS = trace(A).

(b) ≈ one independent piece of information → essentially a column, weighted towards the mid-troposphere (≈ 3–8 km) where thermal contrast with the surface is largest; little near the surface.

(c) Smooth the model profile with the AKs: x_m,smoothed = x_a + A(x_model − x_a), then compare with x̂ (removes the smoothing and a-priori effects).

Question 6 (2.5 pts) — review Ch. 6

(a) Define the aerosol optical depth and the Ångström exponent. What does an Ångström exponent of 1.8 tell you about the particles?

(b) Why is it easier to retrieve AOD over the ocean than over a desert in the visible? Which strategy is used over bright surfaces?

(c) Compare MODIS (passive) and CALIOP (lidar) for studying a Saharan dust plume over the Atlantic.

Show suggested answer key

(a) AOD = ∫ k_ext,aer dz; τ(λ) ∝ λ^(−α). α ≈ 1.8 → fine particles (smoke, urban/sulfate).

(b) Dark ocean → aerosol reflectance dominates TOA signal; bright desert masks the aerosol contribution. Use deep blue (412 nm, dark surfaces), multi-angle/polarisation, or TIR.

(c) MODIS: wide swath, daily AOD map, size info, but no altitude, daytime only. CALIOP: vertical profile, day+night, depolarisation (non-spherical dust), but narrow curtain, 16-day revisit.

Question 7 (2.5 pts) — review Ch. 7

(a) Compute the size parameter x for (i) an N₂ molecule (r ≈ 0.2 nm), (ii) a sulfate particle r = 0.2 µm, (iii) a dust particle r = 5 µm, all at λ = 0.55 µm. Give the scattering regime of each.

(b) Why is the sky blue but clouds white?

(c) Sketch the phase function in the Rayleigh regime and for a large particle.

Show suggested answer key

(a) x = 2πr/λ: (i) ≈ 0.002 → Rayleigh; (ii) ≈ 2.3 → Mie; (iii) ≈ 57 → upper Mie / geometric optics limit.

(b) Molecules: σ ∝ λ⁻⁴ → blue scattered much more. Cloud droplets (x ≫ 1): Q_ext ≈ 2, independent of λ → all colours scattered equally → white.

(c) Rayleigh: ¾(1 + cos²Θ), symmetric forward/back. Large particle: strong forward peak, weak side/back lobes.

Question 8 (2.5 pts) — review Ch. 8

(a) Explain why the O₂ A-band (≈ 760 nm) is suitable for retrieving aerosol or cloud height. Why O₂ and not another gas?

(b) In the thermal infrared, a dust layer at 5 km and another at 1 km have the same AOD. Which one produces the larger brightness-temperature depression, and why? When does the method fail?

(c) Name one other passive technique giving plume heights.

Show suggested answer key

(a) O₂ is well mixed with a known profile (follows pressure); a higher scattering layer shortens the photon path → weaker O₂ absorption. The depth/shape of the band ↔ layer pressure.

(b) The 5 km layer: colder (≈ 6.5 K/km lapse rate) → larger thermal contrast with the surface → larger ΔBT. Fails with low thermal contrast (low layers, night inversions, cold surfaces).

(c) Stereo/multi-angle parallax (MISR); also UV aerosol index dependence on height, O₂–O₂ band at 477 nm.

Question 9 (2.5 pts) — review Part A.1–A.4

(a) Write the radiative transfer equation dI/dl = … in the lecture notation. Explain each term and list the processes that add photons to, or remove photons from, the line of sight.

(b) For a layer of gas without scattering in thermodynamic equilibrium, write the integrated radiative transfer equation and explain its two terms. Include the surface boundary term with emissivity ε_s.

(c) A homogeneous layer, L = 1 km, contains an absorber with N = 5×10¹³ molecules cm⁻³ and σ = 2×10⁻²⁰ cm². Compute τ and the transmission t.

Show suggested answer key

(a) dI(ν̃)/dl = −k_ext·I + J; k_ext = α_gas + s_gas + α_aer + s_aer (extinction); J = source (emission, scattering into the line of sight). Gains: surface reflection, single/multiple scattering into the LOS, emission. Losses: absorption, scattering out of the field of view.

(b) J = α·B(T); I(L) = I(0)e^(−τ(0,L)) + ∫ B(T(l)) e^(−τ(l,L)) dτ: attenuated background + emission of each layer attenuated by the gas above. I(0) = ε_s B(T_s) + (1 − ε_s) I_s, 1 − ε_s = surface albedo.

(c) τ = σ·N·L = 2×10⁻²⁰ × 5×10¹³ × 10⁵ = 0.1 → t = e^(−0.1) ≈ 0.90 (90 %).

Question 10 (2.5 pts) — review Part A.5–A.6

(a) Give the physical origin and the dependence on T and P of the Doppler and Lorentz line widths. Which one dominates at 5 km and at 40 km?

(b) Explain why the variation of line widths with altitude provides information on the vertical distribution of an absorber.

(c) Explain the principle of DOAS using σ = σ_b + σ′. Why is a polynomial included in the fit? If σ′ = 2×10⁻¹⁹ cm² and the slant column is 1×10¹⁶ molecules cm⁻², what is the differential optical depth?

Show suggested answer key

(a) Doppler: thermal motion, Gaussian, ∝ ν̃ √(T/m), independent of P. Lorentz: collisions, ∝ P(T₀/T)ⁿ. Lorentz dominates at 5 km (high P), Doppler at 40 km (P ≈ 3 hPa).

(b) P(z) is known and decreases exponentially → line shape differs for each altitude: centre formed by high layers, wings by low layers; fitting the shape (with known P, T profiles) gives the profile.

(c) Fast structures σ′ = gas fingerprint, slow part σ_b mixed with Rayleigh/Mie/surface → removed by the polynomial. D = σ′·SCD = 2×10⁻¹⁹ × 10¹⁶ = 2×10⁻³ (0.2 % signal).
