Question 1 (2.5 pts) — review Ch. 1

(a) Define the atmospheric lifetime τ of a pollutant. How are lifetimes combined when several removal processes act in parallel? A C1

(b) The rate constant of CO + OH is k ≈ 2×10⁻¹³ cm³ molecule⁻¹ s⁻¹ and the mean OH concentration is ≈ 1×10⁶ molecule cm⁻³. Estimate the lifetime of CO with respect to OH (give the result in days). A C1

(c) A TROPOMI map shows NO₂ concentrated in small hotspots over cities, while a map of CH₄ from the same instrument is almost uniform. Explain the difference. A C1

Show suggested answer key

(a) τ = burden / loss rate (M / L); for first-order sinks τ = 1/k′. Parallel sinks: 1/τ = Σ 1/τᵢ (chemistry, photolysis, dry and wet deposition).

(b) τ = 1/(k[OH]) = 1/(2×10⁻¹³ × 1×10⁶) = 5×10⁶ s ≈ 58 days ≈ 2 months.

(c) NO₂ lives hours → destroyed before transport spreads it → map = sources. CH₄ lives ~10 years ≫ global mixing time (~1 yr) → well mixed, variations of ~1 %.

Question 2 (2.5 pts) — review Ch. 2

(a) Write the Beer–Lambert law for a beam crossing an absorbing and scattering medium. Define optical depth and transmittance. A F1 R1

(b) Explain Kirchhoff’s law and why it matters for thermal-infrared remote sensing. A R2

(c) A beam crosses a layer with vertical optical depth τ = 0.5 at a solar zenith angle of 60°. What fraction of the direct beam is transmitted? A F1

Show suggested answer key

(a) dI = −k_e I ds, I = I₀ e^(−τ), τ = ∫(k_a + k_s) ds = ∫ Σσᵢnᵢ ds; transmittance T = I/I₀ = e^(−τ).

(b) In LTE, emissivity = absorptivity at each wavelength (ε_λ = α_λ): a gas that absorbs at λ also emits B_λ(T) there → TIR spectra carry information on both concentration and temperature.

(c) Slant τ = 0.5 / cos 60° = 1.0 → T = e^(−1) ≈ 0.37 (37 %).

Question 3 (2.5 pts) — review Ch. 3

(a) Explain the three main steps of a DOAS retrieval of the tropospheric NO₂ column from a nadir UV–visible spectrometer. B C3 F1 K1

(b) Why does the air mass factor depend on the a priori shape of the vertical profile and on clouds? B K3

(c) Give two physical reasons why a nadir sounder can obtain some information on the vertical distribution of a gas. B K2 R3

Show suggested answer key

(a) 1) Spectral fit of ln(I₀/I) with lab cross-sections + polynomial → slant column SCD. 2) Remove stratospheric part. 3) Divide by tropospheric AMF (RT model) → VCD.

(b) AMF = SCD/VCD measures the light-path length; sensitivity varies with altitude (low near surface, shielded below cloud, enhanced above) → the result depends on where the gas is assumed to be.

(c) Absorption strength varies with λ (different penetration depths); pressure broadening of lines (TIR); temperature contrast; (limb geometry for limb sounders).

Question 4 (2.5 pts) — review Ch. 4

(a) Sketch the nadir, limb and solar occultation geometries. B K2

(b) Which geometry would you choose to (i) monitor long-term trends of stratospheric ozone with the highest accuracy, (ii) map NO₂ emissions from individual cities? Justify. B K2

(c) What does a geostationary orbit add compared to a low-Earth sun-synchronous orbit for air-quality monitoring? C K2

Show suggested answer key

(a) See Figure “Nadir, limb and occultation viewing geometries” in the study guide (Figure 13).

(b) (i) Solar occultation: self-calibrating (I/I₀), high vertical resolution → trend quality. (ii) Nadir: km-scale horizontal resolution, daily global coverage, sensitive to the boundary layer.

(c) Hourly revisit over one continent → diurnal cycle of pollution (rush hours, photochemistry), more cloud-free scenes. LEO: one overpass per day at fixed local time, but global.

Question 5 (2.5 pts) — review Ch. 5

(a) Write the relation between the retrieved profile, the true profile and the a priori using the averaging kernel matrix. A F3

(b) A retrieval of CO from a thermal-infrared sounder has DOFS = 1.2. What does this mean? Where in the atmosphere is the sensitivity usually highest, and why? B K3 F3

(c) Explain how averaging kernels must be used when comparing satellite retrievals with a chemistry-transport model. A K3 F3

Show suggested answer key

(a) x̂ = x_a + A(x_true − x_a) + Gε; A = ∂x̂/∂x = GK; DOFS = trace(A).

(b) ≈ one independent piece of information → essentially a column, weighted towards the mid-troposphere (≈ 3–8 km) where thermal contrast with the surface is largest; little near the surface.

(c) Smooth the model profile with the AKs: x_m,smoothed = x_a + A(x_model − x_a), then compare with x̂ (removes the smoothing and a-priori effects).

Question 6 (2.5 pts) — review Ch. 6

(a) Define the aerosol optical depth and the Ångström exponent. What does an Ångström exponent of 1.8 tell you about the particles? A F1 C2

(b) Why is it easier to retrieve AOD over the ocean than over a desert in the visible? Which strategy is used over bright surfaces? B C2 R1

(c) Compare MODIS (passive) and CALIOP (lidar) for studying a Saharan dust plume over the Atlantic. B K2

Show suggested answer key

(a) AOD = ∫ k_ext,aer dz; τ(λ) ∝ λ^(−α). α ≈ 1.8 → fine particles (smoke, urban/sulfate).

(b) Dark ocean → aerosol reflectance dominates TOA signal; bright desert masks the aerosol contribution. Use deep blue (412 nm, dark surfaces), multi-angle/polarisation, or TIR.

(c) MODIS: wide swath, daily AOD map, size info, but no altitude, daytime only. CALIOP: vertical profile, day+night, depolarisation (non-spherical dust), but narrow curtain, 16-day revisit.

Question 7 (2.5 pts) — review Ch. 7

(a) Compute the size parameter x for (i) an N₂ molecule (r ≈ 0.2 nm), (ii) a sulfate particle r = 0.2 µm, (iii) a dust particle r = 5 µm, all at λ = 0.55 µm. Give the scattering regime of each. A C2

(b) Why is the sky blue but clouds white? A C2

(c) Sketch the phase function in the Rayleigh regime and for a large particle. B C2

Show suggested answer key

(a) x = 2πr/λ: (i) ≈ 0.002 → Rayleigh; (ii) ≈ 2.3 → Mie; (iii) ≈ 57 → upper Mie / geometric optics limit.

(b) Molecules: σ ∝ λ⁻⁴ → blue scattered much more. Cloud droplets (x ≫ 1): Q_ext ≈ 2, independent of λ → all colours scattered equally → white.

(c) Rayleigh: ¾(1 + cos²Θ), symmetric forward/back. Large particle: strong forward peak, weak side/back lobes.

Question 8 (2.5 pts) — review Ch. 8

(a) Explain why the O₂ A-band (≈ 760 nm) is suitable for retrieving aerosol or cloud height. Why O₂ and not another gas? B R3 C3

(b) In the thermal infrared, a dust layer at 5 km and another at 1 km have the same AOD. Which one produces the larger brightness-temperature depression, and why? When does the method fail? B R2 R3

(c) Name one other passive technique giving plume heights. C K2

Show suggested answer key

(a) O₂ is well mixed with a known profile (follows pressure); a higher scattering layer shortens the photon path → weaker O₂ absorption. The depth/shape of the band ↔ layer pressure.

(b) The 5 km layer: colder (≈ 6.5 K/km lapse rate) → larger thermal contrast with the surface → larger ΔBT. Fails with low thermal contrast (low layers, night inversions, cold surfaces).

(c) Stereo/multi-angle parallax (MISR); also UV aerosol index dependence on height, O₂–O₂ band at 477 nm.

Question 9 (2.5 pts) — review Part A.1–A.4

(a) Write the radiative transfer equation dI/dl = … in the lecture notation. Explain each term and list the processes that add photons to, or remove photons from, the line of sight. A R1 F2

(b) For a layer of gas without scattering in thermodynamic equilibrium, write the integrated radiative transfer equation and explain its two terms. Include the surface boundary term with emissivity ε_s. A F2 R2

(c) A homogeneous layer, L = 1 km, contains an absorber with N = 5×10¹³ molecules cm⁻³ and σ = 2×10⁻²⁰ cm². Compute τ and the transmission t. A F1

Show suggested answer key

(a) dI(ν̃)/dl = −k_ext·I + J; k_ext = α_gas + s_gas + α_aer + s_aer (extinction); J = source (emission, scattering into the line of sight). Gains: surface reflection, single/multiple scattering into the LOS, emission. Losses: absorption, scattering out of the field of view.

(b) J = α·B(T); I(L) = I(0)e^(−τ(0,L)) + ∫ B(T(l)) e^(−τ(l,L)) dτ: attenuated background + emission of each layer attenuated by the gas above. I(0) = ε_s B(T_s) + (1 − ε_s) I_s, 1 − ε_s = surface albedo.

(c) τ = σ·N·L = 2×10⁻²⁰ × 5×10¹³ × 10⁵ = 0.1 → t = e^(−0.1) ≈ 0.90 (90 %).

Question 10 (2.5 pts) — review Part A.5–A.6

(a) Give the physical origin and the dependence on T and P of the Doppler and Lorentz line widths. Which one dominates at 5 km and at 40 km? B R3

(b) Explain why the variation of line widths with altitude provides information on the vertical distribution of an absorber. A R3

(c) Explain the principle of DOAS using σ = σ_b + σ′. Why is a polynomial included in the fit? If σ′ = 2×10⁻¹⁹ cm² and the slant column is 1×10¹⁶ molecules cm⁻², what is the differential optical depth? A C3 F1

Show suggested answer key

(a) Doppler: thermal motion, Gaussian, ∝ ν̃ √(T/m), independent of P. Lorentz: collisions, ∝ P(T₀/T)ⁿ. Lorentz dominates at 5 km (high P), Doppler at 40 km (P ≈ 3 hPa).

(b) P(z) is known and decreases exponentially → line shape differs for each altitude: centre formed by high layers, wings by low layers; fitting the shape (with known P, T profiles) gives the profile.

(c) Fast structures σ′ = gas fingerprint, slow part σ_b mixed with Rayleigh/Mie/surface → removed by the polynomial. D = σ′·SCD = 2×10⁻¹⁹ × 10¹⁶ = 2×10⁻³ (0.2 % signal).

Question 1 (2.5 pts) — review Part B

(a) State the hydrostatic law and the isothermal pressure profile. Give the scale height H for T = 284 K. A R3

(b) Compute the pressure at 3 km and compare with the observed 700 hPa. A R3

(c) Explain how the pressure profile gives information on altitude in a spectrum. B R3 C3

Show suggested answer key

(a) dp/dz = −ρg, ρ = pM/(RT) ⇒ p = p₀exp(−z/H), H = RT/(Mg) = 8.314×284/(29×10⁻³×9.81) ≈ 8.3 km.

(b) p(3 km) = 1013.25·exp(−3/8.30) = 706 hPa; observed 700 hPa (error 0.8%).

(c) Pressure broadening (Lorentz HWHM ∝ p) makes line wings come from low altitude and line centres from high altitude; the O₂ A band uses the known O₂ profile.

Question 2 (2.5 pts) — review Part A

(b) A layer has vertical optical depth 0.3 and the Sun is at a zenith angle of 45°. Compute the slant optical depth and the direct transmission. A F1

Question 3 (2.5 pts) — review Part A

(a) List three gains and two losses of photons along the line of sight. A R1

(b) Does scattering destroy photons? Justify. A R1

(c) For an opaque gas (τ ≫ 1) and for a transparent gas (τ ≪ 1), what does a thermal-IR sensor see? A F2

Show suggested answer key

(a) Gains: surface reflection, scattering into the line of sight (single, multiple), thermal emission. Losses: absorption, scattering out of the field of view.

(b) No: scattering redirects a photon (loss for one direction, gain for another). Absorption converts the energy into heat.

(c) Opaque: only the top layers (e^(−τ(l,L)) small below), saturation. Transparent: I ≈ I(0) + τB, a small emission added to the background.

Question 4 (2.5 pts) — review Chapter 2

(a) State Kirchhoff’s law and the condition for its validity. A R2

(b) Compute the wavelength of maximum emission of the Sun (5778 K) and the Earth (288 K). A R2

(c) Why does thermal-IR sounding need a thermal contrast between the layer and its background? A R2 F2

Show suggested answer key

(a) ε_λ = α_λ in local thermodynamic equilibrium: what absorbs at λ also emits B_λ(T) there.

Question 5 (2.5 pts) — review Chapter 1

(a) Define the atmospheric lifetime and the rule for several sinks. A C1

(b) A pollutant in a 50 km city is removed by chemistry (τ = 6 h), rain once every 3 days and outflow with a 10 km/h wind. Compute the total loss rate, the lifetime and the share of each pathway. A C1

(c) What does this lifetime imply for the satellite map of this pollutant? A C1

(b) k_chem = 4.0 d⁻¹, k_dep = 0.33 d⁻¹, τ_out = 5 h ⇒ k_out = 4.8 d⁻¹; k_tot = 9.13 d⁻¹ ⇒ τ = 2.6 h; shares: outflow 53%, chemistry 44%, rain 4%.

Question 6 (2.5 pts) — review Part B

(a) Write the NOx cycle that produces ozone in the troposphere and give the wavelength threshold. A K1 R1

(b) Why is there no net ozone production without VOC, and what changes this? B R1

(c) Explain NOx-limited and VOC-limited regimes and titration. B C1

Show suggested answer key

(a) NO₂ + hν (λ < 424 nm) → NO + O; O + O₂ + M → O₃; NO + O₃ → NO₂ + O₂.

(b) NO re-destroys the ozone formed (zero balance); peroxy radicals RO₂/HO₂ from VOC oxidise NO to NO₂ without using O₃ ⇒ net production.

(c) NO-poor air: NOx ↑ ⇒ O₃ ↑ (NOx-limited). NO-rich air (near sources): NOx ↑ ⇒ radical loss and titration NO + O₃ ⇒ O₃ ↓ (VOC-limited).

Question 7 (2.5 pts) — review Chapter 7

(b) By what factor is 400 nm light scattered more than 650 nm light by air molecules? A C2

(a) Derive the DIAL expression for the water-vapour extinction coefficient. B C3 F1

(b) P_ON = 12 and 9 nW, P_OFF = 12 and 10 nW at r₁ and r₂ = r₁ + 300 m. Compute α and q (q = 5×10⁴ α). A C3
