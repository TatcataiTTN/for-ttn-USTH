The 12 cards

Key points — what the course is about

K1From water constituents to the colour of the sea

The ocean is a mixture of water, phytoplankton, non-algal particles (NAP), dissolved organic matter (CDOM) and bubbles. Each one sets the inherent optical properties (a, b, β) → they set the light field (AOPs, reflectance) → that is the ocean-colour signal → biogeochemical variables (Chl, POC, CDOM) come back by inversion. (Slides 3, 5, 8, 9; the AOP link is textbook only.)

In 20 seconds: We never measure Chl from space. We measure light leaving the sea; the IOPs link that light to what is in the water.

Typical trap: Saying ocean colour is “the reflection of the sea surface” (slide 7 says it is not).

Go deeper: A · Light in water & IOPs · G · Light field (textbook). Slides: 3, 5, 7, 8, 9

K2Every product rests on a hypothesis

Relations between IOPs and Chl, POC or CDOM are statistical (spread of a factor of 2–3), valid for a water type, a region or a season (slides 65–66, 72–77). Case 1 / Case 2 and the optical water classes (slides 14–16) are the hypotheses that decide which relation to use.

In 20 seconds: Before using an algorithm, say which water type it assumes and how large the spread around the relation is.

Typical trap: Using a Case-1 Chl relation in a river plume.

Go deeper: A · Light in water & IOPs · F · IOPs and biogeochemistry. Slides: 14, 15, 16, 65, 66, 75, 76

K3The ocean is under-sampled: autonomous platforms

Ships and satellites leave huge gaps in space and time (slides 2, 4, 85); floats, gliders and BGC-Argo add the vertical dimension, work under clouds and in winter, and make seven contributions (slide 101). They carry bio-optical sensors: Chl and CDOM fluorescence, bbp, beam-c, irradiance (slides 97–98, 115).

In 20 seconds: Satellites see the surface layer when it is clear; platforms see the interior all year.

Typical trap: Treating fluorescence as Chl without correcting non-photochemical quenching (slide 80).

Go deeper: H · New platforms · F · IOPs and biogeochemistry. Slides: 2, 4, 101, 102, 115

Formulas — the maths you must be able to write

F1Radiative transfer: c = a + b

dL/dr = −c L + ∫β L dω′ + S, c = a + b (slide 5, written with a 2π factor there). A collimated beam: L = L₀ e−c r. b = 2π∫β(θ)sinθ dθ = bf + bb.

In 20 seconds: Light is lost by absorption and by scattering out of the beam, and gained by scattering in from other directions.

Typical trap: Forgetting that c is attenuation (a + b), not absorption; e.g. using a instead of c in Beer–Lambert for a transmissometer.

Go deeper: A · Light in water & IOPs · E · Measuring IOPs. Slides: 5, 17, 18

F2IOP = Σ (N/V)·Q·S

IOP(λ) = Σi (Ni/V) σi(λ) = Σi (Ni/V) Qi(λ) Si (slide 63): number per m³ × efficiency factor × geometric area. Large particles: ∝ area; small particles: ∝ volume (slide 64).

In 20 seconds: A property of the water is the sum over particle classes of number × cross-section.

Typical trap: Mixing σ (m²) and Q (no unit).

Go deeper: F · IOPs and biogeochemistry · D · Particle scattering. Slides: 63, 64

F3Spectral laws: exponential and power law

acdom(λ) = acdom(λ₀) exp[−S′(λ−λ₀)], S′ ≈ 0.014 nm⁻¹ (slide 41); same form for NAP (S ≈ 0.011, slide 35). bp(λ) = bp(550)(λ/550)n, n between 0 and −3, mean ≈ −1 (slide 49). Pure water: βw ∝ λ−4.32 or λ−4.14 (slides 20, 23).

In 20 seconds: Dissolved and detrital material fall exponentially toward the red; particle scattering follows a weak power law; molecular scattering a strong one.

Typical trap: Writing λ₀ in nm for S′ in µm⁻¹, or forgetting the minus sign.

Go deeper: B · Pure water · C · Absorption · D · Particle scattering. Slides: 20, 23, 35, 41, 49

Concepts — the ideas you must be able to explain

C1IOP versus AOP

IOPs (a, b, β, c) depend only on the medium, add up over constituents and do not depend on illumination. AOPs (Kd, R, Rrs) depend on the medium and on the light field (Sun, sky, depth) but are stable enough to describe a water body (textbook).

In 20 seconds: IOPs are what the water is; AOPs are what the light does in it.

Typical trap: Calling Ed an AOP: it changes by a factor of 10 when a cloud passes (textbook).

Go deeper: A · Light in water & IOPs · G · Light field (textbook). Slides: 17, 19

C2Case 1, Case 2 and optical water classes

Case 1: optical properties set mainly by phytoplankton and co-varying CDOM and detritus. Case 2: everything else (mineral particles, CDOM or bubbles not co-varying with Chl). Morel & Prieur 1977 (slide 14). Optical water classes (17 classes, slide 16) describe the continuous variability better than two cases.

In 20 seconds: Case 1/2 is a statement about co-variation, not about depth or distance to the coast.

Typical trap: “Case 2 = coastal”: slide 15 shows Case 2 also at high latitudes.

Go deeper: A · Light in water & IOPs · F · IOPs and biogeochemistry. Slides: 14, 15, 16

C3Package (discretisation) effect

Pigments inside a big cell shade each other: Q*a = aφ/asol falls from 1 (no packaging) toward 0 (maximal) (slide 33). That is why the Chl-specific absorption a*ph drops by up to a factor of 4 from low to high Chl (slides 32–33).

In 20 seconds: A big cell absorbs less per unit of pigment than the same pigment dispersed in solution.

Typical trap: Forgetting that photoacclimation (slide 34) also changes a*ph.

Go deeper: C · Absorption · F · IOPs and biogeochemistry. Slides: 32, 33, 34

Principles — the laws behind everything

R1Additivity

a = aw + aCDOM + aph + aNAP, b = bw + bp, bb = bbw + bbp (slide 19). It allows separate models for each constituent and is the basis of every bio-optical model.

In 20 seconds: Because IOPs add up, we can model each constituent alone and sum.

Typical trap: Adding AOPs (they are not additive).

Go deeper: A · Light in water & IOPs · C · Absorption · D · Particle scattering. Slides: 19, 45, 58

R2Each constituent has a spectral fingerprint

Water: absorption rises beyond 550 nm (slide 24); phytoplankton: blue (≈ 440) and red (≈ 675) peaks (slide 29); CDOM/NAP: exponential decay with λ (slide 41); particles: weak spectral dependence (slides 49–52). The shape of the spectrum identifies who absorbs or scatters.

In 20 seconds: Read the spectrum: slope = CDOM/NAP, peaks = pigments, flat = particles, steep = molecules.

Typical trap: Reading amplitude as if it were shape.

Go deeper: B · Pure water · C · Absorption · D · Particle scattering. Slides: 24, 29, 41, 49

R3Size and refractive index control particle scattering

Large particles scatter mainly by diffraction (forward lobe, ∝ area); small ones by refraction/reflection (Rayleigh, ∝ volume) (slides 47, 64). bbp/bp depends on refractive index first, then size (slide 71): low n (phytoplankton, 1.01–1.09 textbook) gives a small ratio, high n (minerals, 1.15–1.20) a larger one.

In 20 seconds: The backscatter ratio tells you whether particles are organic or mineral.

Typical trap: Assuming bbp/bp is a constant (it varies about 10×).

Go deeper: D · Particle scattering · F · IOPs and biogeochemistry. Slides: 47, 64, 71, 84

Exam checklist — points you must mention

  • I can say the chain K1 and why “colour” is not surface reflection.
  • I can write F1, F2, F3 and define each symbol.
  • I can say IOP vs AOP, Case 1/2 and the package effect.
  • I can name the three principles with an example each.
